# Continental (civil-law) family

Codified systems of continental Europe and those modelled on them: Austria,
Czechia, Germany, Hungary, Poland, Slovakia, and many others.

## Character

- The statute and the code are the primary sources; judges apply and
  interpret them, and openly fill gaps when the code is silent.
- The four classical methods (grammatical, systematic, historical,
  teleological) are the standard toolkit, taught and named as such. Courts
  often cite them by name.
- Many codes contain an interpretive clause (Austria's ABGB §§ 6–7 is the
  classic model) directing courts to the wording, the legislator's clear
  intent, analogy, and finally general principles. Check the overlay for
  the local clause.

## Priority

- The wording is the starting point and the outer boundary of
  interpretation proper; most apex courts call it the "first approach",
  not the last word.
- Objective purpose, read through the system of the law and constitutional
  values, typically outweighs the historical intent of the legislator when
  the two diverge, especially for older codes.
- Departure from clear wording through analogy or teleological reduction is
  recognised in private law and practised by apex courts; it is barred
  against the accused in criminal law and treated restrictively in tax and
  public law.
- Constitutional courts police interpretation: an ordinary court's
  arbitrary or excessively formalistic reading can itself violate the right
  to a fair trial. Constitution-conforming interpretation is required
  before a statute is referred for annulment.
- EU member states add EU-conforming interpretation of national law,
  especially law implementing directives.

## Precedent

- Decisions bind formally only in the case decided, but apex court case law
  has strong persuasive authority. Several systems give it more: unifying
  opinions of supreme court plenaries or grand chambers, or constitutional
  court rulings with general binding effect. Lower courts that depart are
  expected to give reasons and are usually reversed.
- Scholarly commentary carries real weight; courts cite leading
  commentaries and treatises.

## Sources of legislative history

Explanatory memoranda (government reasoning attached to bills), committee
reports, and parliamentary records; most are published officially. The
memorandum explains the government's draft, not necessarily what
parliament enacted after amendments; check whether the provision changed
during passage.
