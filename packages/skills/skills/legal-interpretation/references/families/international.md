# International law: treaties

Treaties are interpreted under the Vienna Convention on the Law of
Treaties (1969), Articles 31 to 33, which also state customary
international law and so apply to states not party to it.

## The general rule (Art. 31)

Read the treaty in good faith, in accordance with the ordinary meaning of
its terms, in their context, and in the light of its object and purpose.
These elements form a single combined operation, not a ranked sequence.

- **Context** includes the text, preamble, and annexes, plus agreements and
  instruments made by the parties in connection with the treaty's
  conclusion.
- **Taken into account with the context:** later agreements between the
  parties on interpretation, later practice that establishes their
  agreement, and relevant rules of international law applicable between
  them.
- **Special meaning** applies if the parties intended it.

## Supplementary means (Art. 32)

Preparatory work and the circumstances of conclusion may confirm a meaning
reached under Art. 31, or determine it when Art. 31 leaves it ambiguous or
obscure, or leads to a manifestly absurd or unreasonable result.

## Languages (Art. 33)

Each authentic text is equally authoritative; the terms are presumed to
have the same meaning in each. Where comparison reveals a difference that
Arts. 31 and 32 cannot remove, adopt the meaning that best reconciles the
texts, having regard to the object and purpose.

## Human-rights treaties

Courts treat them as living instruments read in the light of present-day
conditions, with autonomous concepts that do not depend on national
classification, and read so that rights are practical and effective. The
European Court of Human Rights is the leading example.

## Treaties in national law

How a treaty applies domestically (direct application, priority over
statutes, conforming interpretation) is a question of each state's
constitution; see the jurisdiction overlay.
