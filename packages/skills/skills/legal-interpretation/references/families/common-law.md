# Common-law family

England and Wales, the United States, Ireland, Canada, Australia, India,
and other systems descended from English law.

## Character

- Case law is a primary source; statutes are read against the background
  of the common law they modify.
- Interpretation is described through canons (named presumptions) rather
  than the four continental methods, but the same four arguments sit
  underneath: the canons are linguistic, structural, or purposive, and
  legislative history is a separate, contested source.

## Approaches

- **Textualism** reads the words as a reasonable reader would have
  understood them at enactment, in their statutory context, and gives
  little or no weight to legislative history.
- **Purposivism** asks what problem the statute addressed and reads the
  words to serve that purpose within their linguistic range.
- **Historical English rules:** the literal rule; the golden rule (depart
  from the literal meaning to avoid absurdity); the mischief rule (identify
  the defect the statute was meant to cure). Modern English courts describe
  a single contextual and purposive approach.

## Canons

- Linguistic: ordinary meaning, *noscitur a sociis*, *ejusdem generis*,
  *expressio unius*, consistent usage, the last-antecedent rule.
- Structural: the whole-act rule, the presumption against surplusage, the
  specific governs the general, harmony with related statutes.
- Substantive presumptions: against retroactivity, against
  extraterritoriality, against implied repeal, in favour of reading statutes
  consistently with constitutional limits (constitutional avoidance) and
  with international obligations; the rule of lenity in criminal law.

## Precedent

- *Stare decisis*: decisions of higher courts bind lower courts in the same
  hierarchy; apex courts can overrule themselves but rarely do. Identify
  the binding *ratio decidendi* and separate it from *obiter dicta*.
- A court's construction of a statute becomes part of the statute's meaning
  for later courts in that hierarchy.

## Legislative history

Its use varies by system: freely used but discounted by textualists in the
US; restricted in England to clear ministerial statements on an ambiguous
provision (*Pepper v Hart*); consulted for context in other systems. Say
which rule applies before relying on it.
