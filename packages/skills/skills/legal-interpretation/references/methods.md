# Methods of interpretation

Four kinds of argument recur in every legal tradition: language, system,
history, and purpose. Traditions differ in the weight they give each and in
the order in which they consult them (see the family files), not in the
arguments themselves. Run all four on every question; a reading that
survives all four is far stronger than one that rests on a single method.

## 1. Language

The wording sets the range of meanings a provision can bear. Reading a text
beyond that range is no longer interpretation but law-making, which needs
its own justification (see `priority-and-limits.md`).

- **Ordinary meaning.** Start from the meaning a competent reader of the
  text's audience would give the words when the text was enacted, or today
  where the system reads texts dynamically. Technical audiences (tax,
  securities, medical regulation) bring their own ordinary meaning.
- **Definitions win.** A definition in the instrument, or in a law it
  expressly adopts, displaces ordinary meaning within its scope. Check the
  scope clause: definitions often apply "for the purposes of this Part"
  only.
- **Same term, same meaning; different term, different meaning.** Within an
  instrument, a term is presumed to mean the same thing each time it
  appears, and a change of term is presumed to mark a change of meaning.
  The presumption weakens across instruments of different branches or eras.
- **One word, one meaning per provision.** Do not give a single occurrence
  of a word two meanings to reach a result.
- **Grammar and syntax.** Conjunctions ("and" versus "or"), punctuation,
  the reach of a qualifier over a list, singular and plural, and mood
  ("shall" versus "may") all carry meaning. Note them explicitly.
- **Words read together.** A word takes colour from its neighbours
  (*noscitur a sociis*); a general word after a list of specific ones is
  read as covering things of the same kind (*ejusdem generis*). These are
  aids to the semantic range, not rules of law.
- **Ambiguity versus vagueness.** Ambiguity is a choice between distinct
  meanings, usually resolved by context. Vagueness is a fuzzy boundary,
  usually resolved by purpose. Name which one you face.
- **Equally authentic language versions.** Where several versions are
  authentic (EU law, many treaties, bilingual states), no version controls
  alone. Compare them; where they diverge, resolve by context and purpose.

## 2. System

A provision is part of a legal order presumed to be ordered, consistent,
complete, and free of waste. Systematic arguments come in three strengths.

**Hierarchy (strongest).** Read every norm in conformity with the norms
above it: the constitution and its fundamental rights; for EU member
states, EU law; ratified human-rights treaties and other binding
international obligations; for subordinate legislation, the statute that
authorises it. Where several readings exist, choose one that conforms. A
conforming reading is preferred over striking the norm down, but it cannot
contradict the wording outright.

**Coherence.**

- **No contradiction.** Avoid readings under which the same conduct is both
  required and forbidden, or both forbidden and permitted.
- **Collision rules** for a genuine conflict that interpretation cannot
  remove: the higher norm prevails (*lex superior*); the specific prevails
  over the general (*lex specialis*); the later prevails over the earlier of
  equal rank (*lex posterior*), but a later general rule does not, without
  more, displace an earlier specific one.
- **Principles as optimisation.** When two legal principles pull apart,
  prefer the reading that realises both as fully as possible rather than
  sacrificing one entirely.
- **Stated principles of the instrument.** Many codes open with their own
  guiding principles or interpretive clause; read the operative provisions
  consistently with them.
- **Unity of concepts across the order.** A concept shared by several laws
  (good faith, legal person, damage, decision) is presumed to carry its
  general meaning unless a branch's own principles call for a different
  one. Private law and public law, or criminal and civil law, may
  legitimately diverge.

**Structure and economy (weakest; drafting conventions).**

- **No surplus, no dead letters.** Prefer readings that give every word and
  provision some work to do, and avoid readings that make a provision
  inapplicable.
- **Express mention of one thing can exclude others** (*expressio unius*,
  argument *a contrario*), but only where the list is meant to be closed.
- **Avoid creating gaps** that the system did not intend; but see
  `priority-and-limits.md` on when a gap is real.
- **Placement and headings** (argument *a rubrica*): where a provision sits,
  and the heading over it, are evidence of its scope.
- **Logical arguments:** *a fortiori* (if the law covers the weaker case it
  covers the stronger), *a maiore ad minus*, *a minore ad maius*. State the
  form you use.

## 3. History

What the author actually meant, read from the record of the text's making.

- **Sources.** Explanatory memoranda, committee reports, recorded debate,
  travaux préparatoires, recitals and preambles, earlier drafts; for
  contracts, negotiations and prior dealings, where the governing law
  admits them.
- **Collective author.** A legislature's intent is the shared intent the
  record shows, not one member's view. Statements by the sponsor or the
  government carry more weight than a backbencher's speech; a statement
  contradicted in the same debate carries little.
- **Rejected alternatives.** If the author considered and rejected a
  wording, do not reach the rejected result by interpretation.
- **Occasion.** The problem the law answered (*occasio legis*, the
  "mischief") explains its scope.
- **Evolution.** Compare the provision with its predecessors and later
  amendments: a change of wording signals a change of rule; a re-enactment
  without change can signal endorsement of settled case law.
- **Law not yet in force.** An enacted but not yet effective provision can
  inform the reading of the current one where both pursue the same aim.
- **Weight.** History confirms or corrects; it rarely decides alone, and
  some systems discount it heavily (see the family files).

## 4. Purpose

What the provision is for, and what the legal order as a whole demands of
it.

- **Purpose of the norm.** Identify the concrete aim of this provision,
  then the purpose of the legal institution it belongs to, then the guiding
  principles of its branch, then the constitutional values above them.
  Prefer specific purposes over general ones at the same level, but read
  specific purposes in the light of the general.
- **Subjective and objective purpose.** The author's actual aim (history)
  and the aim a reasonable author would pursue within today's legal order.
  Their relative weight depends on the text:

  | Text                         | Author's actual aim         | Reasonable-author and systemic aim     |
  | ---------------------------- | --------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
  | Will                         | dominant                    | fills gaps                             |
  | Negotiated contract          | heavy (common intent)       | supplements; stronger for standard terms and consumer contracts |
  | Recent, specific statute     | significant                 | important, usually confirms            |
  | Old statute or general code  | supplementary               | dominant                               |
  | Constitution                 | informs                     | dominant                               |
  | Treaty                       | relevant (object and purpose at conclusion) | important (good faith, subsequent practice) |

- **Like cases alike** (*de similibus idem est iudicandum*): provisions
  with the same purpose are read consistently.
- **Settled case law and scholarly consensus.** A consistent line of apex
  court decisions, and the prevailing view in the literature, are evidence
  of how the legal community understands the text and protect legitimate
  expectations. Departing from them needs explicit reasons.
- **Consequences.** Weigh the practical, economic, and social effects of
  each reading. Readings that are unworkable, self-defeating, or plainly
  unjust are suspect.
- **No absurd results** (*reductio ad absurdum*): reject a reading whose
  outcome no reasonable author could have wanted. Use it sparingly and say
  why the result is absurd, not merely undesirable.
- **Comparative law.** How other systems with similar rules read them is a
  supporting argument, strongest where the provision was borrowed or
  harmonised.
- **Freedom in doubt** (*in dubio pro libertate*): where the state limits
  individual liberty and the methods leave real doubt, prefer the reading
  that limits less. Its weight depends on the constitutional order.
- **Accepted standards of conduct.** Good morals, fairness, and good faith
  inform open-textured terms.
- **Balancing.** When purposes, principles, or values collide, weigh them
  openly; constitutional values outrank the rest and are balanced only
  against each other.
- **Gap-filling.** Analogy (extending a rule to an unregulated case with the
  same reason) and teleological reduction (cutting back a rule whose words
  reach further than its reason) go beyond the wording. Their conditions are
  in `priority-and-limits.md`.
- **Exceptions are read narrowly** (*singularia non sunt extendenda*): an
  exception is not extended by analogy beyond its reason.
