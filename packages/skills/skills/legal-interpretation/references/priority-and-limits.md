# Priority between methods, and the limits of interpretation

No legal system ranks the methods in a fixed order that settles every case,
and courts that claim one rarely follow it. What can be stated are default
rules with known exceptions. The family file and the jurisdiction overlay
adjust these defaults; where they conflict with this file, they win.

## Default rules

1. **Convergence first.** The most persuasive reading is one that language,
   system, history, and purpose all support. Before ranking methods, look
   for a reading that reconciles them; most apparent conflicts dissolve on a
   closer reading of the text or a more precise statement of its purpose.
2. **The text has the first word.** Only the text was enacted and
   published, and the public is entitled to rely on it. A reading supported
   by the wording prevails unless stronger arguments displace it, and the
   burden of argument lies on the reader who departs from it.
3. **Purpose is never ignored.** Even a clear text is checked against its
   purpose; mechanical application that defeats the evident aim is an error,
   not fidelity. Whether purpose can then override the text is a separate
   question (rule 7).
4. **Higher-law conformity is mandatory.** Among the readings the text
   allows, one that conforms to the constitution, binding EU law, or binding
   international obligations must be chosen. If none conforms, the norm is
   set aside or referred for review under the system's own procedure; it is
   not quietly rewritten.
5. **Settled case law has presumptive weight.** A consistent line of apex
   court decisions is followed unless there are convincing, stated reasons
   to depart. The strength of this presumption varies sharply: binding
   precedent in common-law systems, strongly persuasive in most continental
   systems, binding by statute for certain courts in some (see overlays).
6. **History informs, rarely decides.** The record of enactment is weaker
   than the text and, in most continental systems, weaker than a convincing
   objective purpose. It is strongest when it is explicit, uncontradicted,
   and recent. "The legislature made a drafting error" may justify departing
   from the text only with strong support from purpose and system, and never
   to the detriment of a person facing state power.
7. **Departing from clear wording is exceptional.** Where the wording is
   clear, a purpose may prevail over it only if that purpose is evident,
   undisputed, and clearly dominant over any purposes the wording serves.
   The typical cases are a gap (the law fails to regulate a case its reason
   covers: analogy) and an overreach (the words catch a case their reason
   does not: teleological reduction). A result that is absurd on the literal
   reading is the clearest instance.
8. **Two exceptions to rule 7:**
   - **Deliberate gaps.** If the record shows the legislature knowingly left
     the case unregulated, and circumstances have not changed materially
     since, the gap is not filled by interpretation.
   - **Constitutionally barred extensions.** Criminal liability and penalties
     are never extended by analogy or by reading against the wording to the
     accused's detriment. The same restraint applies, in most systems, to
     imposing taxes and to restricting fundamental rights, which require a
     clear legal basis.
9. **Purpose over drafting conventions.** Where the text is genuinely
   ambiguous, a convincing purpose outweighs arguments drawn only from
   drafting habits (no surplus words, headings, placement). It does not
   outweigh hierarchy (rule 4) or coherence arguments that prevent a
   contradiction in the legal order.
10. **Branch matters.** Private law tolerates more gap-filling, because
    analogy between equal parties avoids a denial of justice rather than
    expanding state power. Criminal, administrative-sanction, and tax law
    give the wording more weight. Constitutional law, built from
    principles, gives purpose and balancing more weight.

## How much each argument binds

Treat arguments in four tiers when you weigh them:

- **Mandatory, without exception:** conformity with higher law (rule 4).
- **Mandatory to consider, may be outweighed:** ordinary meaning and
  definitions, avoiding contradiction, the purpose of the norm, settled
  case law.
- **Relevant:** the remaining systematic, historical, and purposive
  arguments; they choose between readings the mandatory arguments leave
  open.
- **Supporting:** comparative law, freedom in doubt (unless the
  constitution makes it mandatory), law not yet in force; use them when the
  stronger arguments leave a tie.

## The limit

Interpretation ends where the wording ends. A result outside every meaning
the words can bear is gap-filling or law-making. Say so explicitly, name the
technique (analogy, teleological reduction, conforming correction), and
check that the jurisdiction and branch permit it. If they do not, the honest
answer is that the text does not reach the result and only the legislature,
or a constitutional court striking the norm down, can.
